01Who we are

SafeRoute is a crime-aware walking navigation app for the United Kingdom, 26 US cities, two Canadian cities (Toronto and Vancouver), and Mexico City. It also publishes a website, safe-route.app, with neighbourhood safety pages and a map for checking any address. The data controller is Minhaj Khan (an individual based in the State of New York, United States), referred to throughout this policy as "we," "us," or "SafeRoute."

Contact: minhaj@safe-route.app

This policy explains what personal information SafeRoute collects, why we collect it, how we use it, and the rights you have under the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) and the UK Data Protection Act 2018, as amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025.

02Scope

This policy applies to the SafeRoute iOS app, the optional SafeRoute Apple Watch companion, the SafeRoute website at safe-route.app (including its address-check map and neighbourhood safety pages), this policy page, and the SafeRoute backend service that supports them, including the page it shows to anyone who opens a link to a walk you share (section 03). 03Information we collect

SafeRoute is designed to collect as little personal information as possible. We do not require accounts, sign-in, or registration. We do not ask for your name, postal address, phone number, or payment information; if you type an address into a search box, it is handled as described below, and if you share a walk, so is the name or address of its destination.

We process the following limited categories of data while you use the app or the website:

Location data (GPS coordinates)

When you actively use SafeRoute to plan or follow a route, the app reads your device's GPS location. Coordinates are sent to our backend to compute walking routes, to retrieve crime context for your area and, if you use Share my walk, to show your walk to the people you send the link to (see "Share my walk" below). When you navigate, the app also sends your route, and in some cases your position, straight to Mapbox. To name the area you are in, the app also sends your location to Apple's map services. Both are described under "The app's map" below. Coordinates are not stored against any persistent identifier on our servers; if you share a walk, its route and positions are held for a few hours under a random code, not under any identifier of yours. When the app checks your area, looks for help nearby, or ranks search results near you, your device's current location travels in the address of the request, and our request log keeps it only to about 1 km (see "Technical request data" below). Our cache of crime data keeps the box around the point of an area check, and around a route you plan, to about 10 m for up to a day, or longer in Dallas (section 08).

Three features can send your location to our backend without you opening the app; since version 1.3.8, released on 1 October 2026, Area heads-up no longer does:

  • Home Screen widget: if you add the SafeRoute widget to your Home Screen or Lock Screen, it asks our backend for an update about every 30 minutes (iOS decides exactly when), sending the last location the app recorded, if that is less than a day old. Since version 1.3.8, released on 1 October 2026, the widget first rounds that location to about 100 m. It then sends nothing to our backend, but shows its last answer again, while the rounded point is within about 100 m of the one that answer was for, that answer gave a risk level (not, for example, "No data here") and is less than 6 hours old, and the app has not checked an area since. Earlier versions send the location unrounded at every update.
  • Area heads-up: if you turn on these alerts and let SafeRoute use your location Always, iOS watches a few nearby high-incident zones for the app and notices when you have moved some distance. The watching itself sends nothing to our backend, and since version 1.3.8, released on 1 October 2026, the app checks your area for these alerts only when you open it. In earlier versions, iOS can relaunch the app in the background to update the zones, and when it does, the app also checks your area as if you had opened it, which sends your current location to our backend. The alerts do not need this, and version 1.3.8 no longer does it.
  • Fresh incident nearby: if you turn this on (in About, or on the Tonight card in cities that offer it), the app sends its latest known position when iOS wakes it in the background (at most about every 30 minutes), when you open or return to the app, and when you switch the setting on, to check for serious incidents nearby. Since version 1.3.8, released on 1 October 2026, the app rounds that position to about 1 km before sending it; earlier versions send it to about 1 m. Our server cuts it to about 1 km as it arrives and uses it only to find your city's recent calls.

As with any other request, our request log keeps these locations only to about 1 km. You can stop them by removing the widget or by turning the alerts off in About.

Share my walk

This part is written for the person who shares a walk. If you opened a link to someone's walk, the last paragraph of this part says what your browser sends and to whom; section 05 gives our lawful basis, section 08 says how long we keep what it sends, and section 09 sets out your rights.

If you tap Share my walk on a route and then Create link & send, the app sends our backend the name of your destination (a place name or an address), the route you planned and your estimated walking time, and our backend makes a link to a page that shows them. The route is its full line of coordinates: it starts where you planned it from, which is often your current location, and passes through any stops you added. Nothing is sent until you tap Create link & send.

If you choose Live location, which is selected unless you change it, the app also sends your precise position, with the time, about every 15 seconds while you navigate, including while your screen is locked. If a send fails, the app tries again with the next position, up to about once a second; if our backend no longer has the walk, for example after it restarts, every later attempt fails, whenever you navigate, until sharing stops in one of the ways below, and our backend rejects those positions without keeping them. From 4 hours after the link was made until we delete the walk (section 08), our backend still acknowledges each position the app sends, but does not keep it, so the last position the page can show is the last one sent before the 4 hours were up. If you choose Route only, the app sends no position. Reaching your destination does not by itself stop sharing: it stops when you end navigation (including from the screen navigation shows when you arrive), when you share another walk, or when you tap Stop sharing on Apple Watch, which appears only while you navigate. With Live location, if you start navigating, even to somewhere else, before sharing has stopped and while SafeRoute has kept running since you made the link, your positions from that navigation are sent to it too.

There is one exception to ending navigation: if you choose Go on a place in Find Help, on your phone or on Apple Watch, navigation ends but sharing does not. The link stays open, and the page keeps saying that you are walking and, later, that you are later than expected; Stop sharing disappears from your watch; and with Live location, your next navigation, for example to that place, sends its positions to the same link. Sharing then stops only when you end a later navigation in the usual way, tap Stop sharing on your watch during one, or share another walk; otherwise the link stays open until about 4 hours 15 minutes after it was made (section 08).

You send the link yourself, with whichever app you pick from iOS's share sheet, and that app handles the message under its own terms; the ready-made message names your destination. The link works as soon as it is made, even if you then close the share sheet without sending it, and we are not told who receives it.

The link holds a random 12-character code, not your name or phone number, but anyone who has it, including anyone it is passed on to, can open the page without a key (section 11). They see your destination, your planned route with its start and end marked, and whether you are still walking or are later than expected, or that sharing has ended (the page then says "Sharing ended" and "The walker ended navigation or stopped sharing"), or, once the link is 4 hours old, that the link is closing and, with Live location, how long ago it last updated. When our backend is told that sharing has stopped, even at your destination, the page says that sharing ended, not that you arrived: it can say that you arrived only if the app tells our backend so, which no released version does (section 13). The page, or the data behind it, also gives your estimated walking time and when the link was made and last updated. With Live location they also see your latest position, and the data behind the page holds the positions our backend has kept for that link, each with its time, up to the last 200 (at least about 50 minutes of walking). Send the link only to people you want to see this.

We hold a shared walk in our backend's memory only and never write it to disk. We delete it about 30 minutes after sharing stops, and never later than about 4 hours 15 minutes after the link was made (section 08). Every shared walk is also lost if our server restarts. Until then the page keeps showing the route and, with Live location, your last position. The ways of stopping above work only while SafeRoute has kept running since you made the link: if it has been closed or restarted, or cannot reach our backend when sharing stops, the link receives no more positions but stays open until it expires. A link you make without then starting navigation can be stopped only by starting and ending navigation or by sharing another walk. Deleting the app does not end a walk you have shared.

The page asks our backend for updates about every 5 seconds for the first 4 hours, until sharing stops, and every 30 seconds after that, and each request reaches our backend, through Cloudflare, with the viewer's IP address (section 06); our request log replaces the code in these links with a placeholder (see "Technical request data" below). The page loads its map straight from Mapbox, so the viewer's browser sends Mapbox its IP address and browser details, the area of map around your route, and usage events carrying a random identifier that Mapbox's map library keeps in that browser; your route, your destination, your positions and the link's code are never sent to Mapbox (section 06). Since 30 September 2026 the page links to this section. Our usage analytics (below) record only that a walk was shared and whether you chose Live location, and nothing about it once you have turned them off.

Search queries

Text you type into the place-search box (for example, a destination name) is sent to our backend, which forwards it to Mapbox's geocoding service to turn it into a map location. If you have allowed location access, the app also sends your current location with each search so that nearby places rank first, and our backend passes it to Mapbox for the same purpose. We do not link your searches to you. The text and any location travel in the address of each request, but our request log keeps only the length of the text and the location cut to about 1 km (see "Technical request data" below).

The app's map

The maps in the app — on the Nearby tab, including its full-screen crime map, on the Live and Plan tabs, and in turn-by-turn navigation — are drawn by Mapbox's software inside the app, which downloads map data directly from Mapbox (api.mapbox.com): the map's style, fonts, icons and map tiles and, while you navigate, terrain, 3D buildings and routing data for the area around you. So Mapbox receives your device's IP address, the app's name, version and build number, the versions of Mapbox's software, your iOS version and device model, and the areas of map you view, which, while you navigate, are the area around you. These requests also carry a billing token from Mapbox's software, part of which stays the same for as long as the app is installed. Searching for places, and finding the routes you compare before you start, do not go from your device to Mapbox: the app sends them to our backend (see "Search queries" above and section 06); only once you navigate does the app ask Mapbox for directions itself, as described below.

Each time a map or a navigation session starts, Mapbox's software also sends Mapbox a usage check-in (to events.mapbox.com), which Mapbox uses for billing, and from time to time it asks Mapbox for its settings (from config.mapbox.com). Both carry the identifier iOS assigns to SafeRoute on your device (Apple's "identifier for vendor"), unhashed, which stays the same until you delete the app; the analytics identifier described below is derived from it. The check-in also gives your device model, iOS version and the app's name and version, whether Location Services is on, and the location access you gave SafeRoute (While Using or Always, and precise or approximate).

When you start navigation, the app sends Mapbox the route you chose — up to 100 points along it, from its start, which is usually where you are, to your destination — with the names of the start and the destination (when you start from where you are, the start is named "Current Location"), your language and your units of distance, so that Mapbox can turn it into turn-by-turn directions. As you walk, the app fetches each spoken instruction from Mapbox by sending its text, which names the streets ahead, with your language. On that route, the app sends Mapbox no position while you walk, even if you leave the route. If Mapbox cannot use the route you chose, the app instead asks Mapbox for a walking route between your start, any stops and your destination, with their names; while you follow that route, it sends Mapbox your current position, at full precision, and your direction of travel whenever you leave the route, to get a new one, and your current position and direction of travel from time to time, to look for a better one. Versions before 1.2.1 always navigate this second way.

SafeRoute switches off Mapbox's own usage telemetry each time it starts, and every version since 1.0.2 (June 2026) has done so. With it off, the Report button that Mapbox's software shows during navigation, and the "Rate your trip" screen it shows when you arrive, send nothing to Mapbox; we checked this with version 1.3.8. Versions 1.0.0 and 1.0.1, from May 2026, do not switch it off, so with them Mapbox's software sends what the list below describes unless you switch it off as described there.

The ⓘ button on each map opens a menu from Mapbox. Its Improve this map item opens Mapbox's map-feedback page in your browser at the place the map shows; the page's address gives that place to about 1 m, and says that you came from SafeRoute. Its Mapbox Telemetry item asks whether you want to take part in Mapbox's telemetry, which Mapbox's dialog describes as "contributing anonymous usage data". It is not anonymous: it carries the identifier described above. If you choose Participate, Mapbox's software sends Mapbox the following until SafeRoute next starts, when the app switches it off again (in versions 1.0.0 and 1.0.1, until you switch it off in the same menu):

  • your precise location each time the app receives it (about once a second while you navigate), with your speed, direction of travel, altitude and the location's accuracy, whether the app is open or in the background, and the location access you gave SafeRoute; only approximate locations if you gave SafeRoute approximate access;
  • events when a map opens, with the identifier described above, your device model, iOS version, screen scale, text-size setting and orientation, and whether you are on Wi-Fi; and counts of how the app uses Mapbox's map software;
  • events when you start, finish or cancel navigation, with your route, your last 20 positions, and your device's battery, volume, screen brightness, audio output and type of connection;
  • anything you send with the Report button or the "Rate your trip" screen: the category you chose or your rating, any comment, word for word, your last 20 positions, your route, and a screenshot of the navigation screen, which shows the map around you and your comment.

You can stop it at any time by choosing Stop Participating in the same menu.

A few things in the app use Apple's own map services (Apple Maps), which iOS provides, rather than Mapbox. To show the name of your area on the Nearby tab, the app asks Apple to turn your current location into a place name. When an alert fires, it asks Apple for the name of the street and area at the alert's point and for a small picture of the map around that point, and the widget asks Apple for a picture of the map of the area it shows. So Apple receives those locations and areas of map from your device, with its IP address.

Motion sensor data

If you use the SafeRoute Apple Watch companion, the watch reads device motion data on-device only, to detect off-route deviations. Motion data is not transmitted to our servers.

Technical request data

For security and abuse prevention, our backend writes one line to its log for every request: the time, the address requested, the response code, the size of the response and how long it took to answer. Before the line is written, any search text in the address is replaced by its length, and any coordinates are cut to two decimal places (about 1 km); the code in a link to a shared walk is replaced by a placeholder, and the rest of the address is kept, such as a camera identifier. That line does not include your IP address. Our host, Render, keeps these logs for about 7 days, and they are not used to build a user profile. Separately, to enforce rate limits, our server counts requests in memory for one to two minutes, keyed on the IP address each request arrives from (see section 08).

Usage analytics

To understand which features are used and to catch performance problems, SafeRoute records a small number of usage signals through TelemetryDeck, a privacy-focused, GDPR-compliant analytics provider. These signals carry an event name (for example, that a screen was opened or a route was found) and a small set of coarse, bucketed values describing what happened — how many routes or search results were returned, or how many incidents are in a part of the heatmap you tap; whether the safest route, or the one you started, was low, moderate or high for reported incidents; which tab you switched to, and which of the routes, by rank, you chose; whether the routes had crime data behind them, whether you started navigation from them, and whether navigation followed the route you chose or one Mapbox made instead; whether an alert setting was switched on or off, why alerts could not work (notifications or location off), and how many times the app had offered alerts; a load-time band; a failure reason; how long the route results were open, to the nearest band; and a coarse region label (for example US, UK, Canada, or Mexico). TelemetryDeck's software also adds to each signal details of your device and settings, including the device model, iOS version, screen size and orientation; your language, region, locale and time-zone settings, light or dark mode, and accessibility settings such as text size, bold text and reduced motion; the app's version and whether it came from the App Store or TestFlight; the date and hour; and the date the app was first used, how many sessions it has had and on how many days, and how long the last session and the average session lasted. It also sends signals of its own when a session starts and when it detects a new installation. These signals never include your search text, coordinates, addresses, routes, or contacts.

They are not, however, wholly identifier-free, and we would rather say so than imply otherwise. TelemetryDeck assigns each installation a pseudonymous client identifier, derived from the vendor identifier iOS gives us by hashing it on your device, with SHA-256 and no secret salt, so anyone who holds the unhashed identifier, as Mapbox does, could compute it. It is stable across sessions on the same device, which is what lets us tell one returning user from ten new ones. It is not your name, it is not shared with anyone, it cannot be reversed into your identity, it is reset if you delete and reinstall the app, and it is never combined with data from other companies. The vendor identifier it is derived from also goes, unhashed, to Mapbox, whose map software in the app sends it each time a map starts (see "The app's map" above); TelemetryDeck receives only the hashed form, and the app never sends the hashed form to Mapbox. TelemetryDeck does not use advertising identifiers and does not track you across other apps or websites. You can turn this off at any time in the app under About → Anonymous analytics. The app then stops recording its own signals at once, and nothing is sent from the next time SafeRoute starts afresh, rather than returning from the background. Until then, TelemetryDeck's software can still send signals the app recorded before you turned it off, and its own signal that a session has started, with the identifier and device details described above, when you come back to the app after more than 5 minutes away.

Beyond the usage analytics described above and the website analytics described below, SafeRoute uses no advertising identifiers and no cross-app trackers, and the app is configured with NSPrivacyTracking = false in its Privacy Manifest.

Where an install came from

We advertise SafeRoute on the App Store, and we need to know whether those ads work. On its first launch, and if it has no answer, on up to four later launches, the app asks iOS for an attribution token using Apple's own AdServices framework and sends it to api-adservices.apple.com, which answers whether this install followed one of our Apple Search Ads. We keep two things from that answer: a single label — appleSearchAds, organic, or unknown if it has asked on five launches without an answer — and, where Apple supplies one, the numeric campaign identifier. Everything else Apple returns is discarded on the device.

This is Apple's own measurement API, not a third-party one. It does not use the advertising identifier (IDFA), it requires no App Tracking Transparency prompt, and it describes the advert rather than the person — so it is not tracking as Apple defines it. The resulting label is recorded as one analytics event, which means it carries the same pseudonymous client identifier described above, and it is silenced entirely if you have turned analytics off.

What is in your ears

To decide whether open-ear audio hardware is worth supporting, the app checks which kind of output your audio is currently routed to and records it as one of five broad categories: built-in speaker, wired, other Bluetooth, open-ear glasses, or other. The device's own name for that output — which is free text you may have personalised — is matched on the phone and discarded immediately. It is never stored, never logged and never transmitted; only the category leaves the device, with whether it was read as navigation started or when the output changed. SafeRoute requests no microphone access and records no audio.

Website analytics

Unless your browser sends a Global Privacy Control signal or you have opted out (see below), every page on the website loads Cloudflare Web Analytics, a cookieless visit counter. When a page loads, and again when you leave it, the script sends Cloudflare:

  • Page: the page address, with anything after a ? or # removed.
  • Where you came from: the page or site that linked you there, if your browser reports one.
  • Browser: your browser's engine and version.
  • Performance: how long the page took to load and draw, how much memory it used, how quickly it responded to a click or tap, and which element on the page was the largest or moved while loading. On the map pages the largest element can be a map image, whose web address names the square of map it shows — for example the wide region /check/ opens on, which it picks from your device's time zone, or, after a search from the home page, a square of map containing the place you searched for.

We look at the results only as totals, such as a daily summary of the most-visited pages, the sites visitors came from, and visitors' countries, with automated traffic excluded.

You can say no to this without contacting us, and nothing on the website depends on it:

  • Global Privacy Control: if your browser sends this signal, the website does not load the counter at all, and stores nothing.
  • Our opt-out switch: you can turn the counter off at safe-route.app/privacy-choices/. This keeps one on/off flag in your browser's local storage for safe-route.app (named sr:analytics-optout), which the website checks before loading the counter on any page. The flag is set by our page, not by Cloudflare. It records only that choice, is the same for everyone who opts out, is never sent to us or to Cloudflare, applies only to that browser, and is removed if you switch the counter back on or clear your browser's data for the site.

Blocking cloudflareinsights.com in your browser or with a content blocker also stops it.

Checking an address on the website

The map at safe-route.app/check/ sends what you type in its search box to our backend as you type — after each short pause, once you have typed two characters — so unfinished text is sent, not only the search you settle on. With each search it normally also sends the point at the centre of the map, rounded to about 100 m, so that places near what you are viewing rank first: before your first lookup, that point is usually in the region the map opened on; after one, it is in the area you are looking at, which may be around your own location. Our backend passes the text and that point to Mapbox to find matching places; Mapbox receives them from our server, not from your browser, and does not get your IP address. A search typed on the home page is normally carried to /check/ after a # in the page address, a part that browsers do not send to any server; /check/ removes it as it opens and then sends it to our backend like any other search. If JavaScript is turned off in your browser, the home-page box instead sends your search to GitHub as part of the page address (/check/?q=…).

When you pick a place or one of the examples, the page rounds that point to about 100 m (three decimal places) and sends it to our backend to fetch the crime summary for the area. Choosing a city under Where it works, or its dot on the map, sends that city centre's fixed coordinates in the same way. After a lookup, moving the map sends the new map centre, rounded in the same way, for the same purpose and to name the area. Our backend handles these as it does for the app: for most cities, a box about 2 km across, centred on the point, goes to that city's crime-data source, and the map centre goes to Mapbox for a place name. A point outside the areas we cover goes to no crime-data source. In the UK, the area we cover is England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the area data.police.uk holds (section 06). For the recent 911 calls, the page then sends only a short key naming the city or area our backend matched (for example seattle), not a point. For the street cameras, it asks which cameras are near the point rounded to about 1 km, and then asks for each still or clip by camera and city alone. Our server sends nothing about you on to those sources, but for the street cameras of New York City's Department of Transportation (NYC DOT), your browser then fetches each picture from NYC DOT itself (see "Street cameras on the website" below).

Nothing is sent to our backend until you search, pick a place or a city, or press the location button. The neighbourhood search boxes on the home page and on the city pages under /safety/ work entirely in your browser and send what you type nowhere. /tonight/ asks our backend for recent 911 calls in seven fixed cities as it opens, which tells us nothing about you beyond what any request carries.

Your location on the website

/check/ asks your browser for your location only when you press its location button, never when the page loads, and no other page on the website asks for it. If you allow it, the page does not ask your browser for its high-accuracy mode, and it rounds the position your browser returns to about 100 m before sending it to our backend to look up that area, as above. If you decline, nothing is sent.

The website's map

The map pages draw their map from images that your browser downloads directly from Mapbox (api.mapbox.com). Each image covers a fixed square of the map, so Mapbox receives your IP address and browser details together with the areas you look at, and the set of squares fetched shows where your view is centred. After a lookup — including one of your own location — the squares range from a few kilometres down to a few hundred metres across, and are smaller still if you zoom in. /check/ opens on a region chosen from your device's time zone; the time zone itself is not sent anywhere.

Street cameras on the website

As in the app, camera stills and London clips on /check/ are fetched by our server, so the transport authority never sees you, except the stills from NYC DOT's street cameras. Your browser asks our backend for each one by camera and city alone, with no point, and each request is written to our request log; while a Las Vegas still is open it is refreshed about once a minute, and each refresh is a new request. For an NYC DOT camera, NYC DOT's server does not answer ours, so our backend answers only with where to fetch the picture, and your browser then loads it directly from NYC DOT (nyctmc.org), each time it loads or refreshes. NYC DOT therefore receives your IP address, your browser details and which camera you opened. Your browser is told not to send a referrer, so NYC DOT is not told which page you were on (section 06). Live video works differently, again as in the app: it is played directly from the video service the camera's authority uses (511NY / NYSDOT for New York, Nevada DOT for Las Vegas), so that service receives your IP address, your browser details and that the request came from safe-route.app. The small video-player library (hls.js) that plays it comes from our own website. And because the still shown while the video starts comes through our server, our server can tell which live camera you opened on the website.

04What we do not collect

  • Your name, email address, phone number, or postal address (an address you type into a search box, or the destination of a walk you share, is handled as described in section 03)
  • Your contacts, photos, calendar, or other on-device content
  • Your device's advertising identifier (IDFA)
  • Browsing history outside of SafeRoute, beyond the single page or site that linked you to our website, if your browser reports it (section 03)
  • Health data, biometrics, or financial information

08How long we keep your information

Deleted about 30 minutes after sharing stops, and never later than about 4 hours 15 minutes after the link was made, so no shared walk is kept for more than about 4 hours 15 minutes. Positions sent from 4 hours after the link was made are not kept. At most the last 200 positions are kept, each with its time: at least about 50 minutes of walking. 11Security

We protect your data using:

  • Transport security: All traffic between the app and our backend uses TLS 1.2 or higher. App Transport Security (ATS) is enforced. The website is served only over HTTPS (plain http:// requests are redirected), and its requests to our backend use HTTPS too.
  • Authentication on backend endpoints: Most of our backend requires a key that is shipped with the app, not entered by users. The read-only endpoints the website uses need no key, because a web page cannot keep one secret, and neither do a few other read-only ones: a health check, the nearby-help lookup, and the pages that let someone follow a walk you have shared. Anyone with the link can open those pages: the link holds a random 12-character code, the pages are not rate-limited, and changes to a walk need a separate secret, which our server keeps with the walk and gives only to your phone.
  • Rate limiting: Most endpoints are rate-limited by the IP address each request arrives from, to prevent abuse; the ones the website uses allow 30 requests a minute.
  • Minimal data collection: The strongest protection is data we never collect.
  • No password storage: SafeRoute has no accounts, so we never store passwords.
  • Server hardening: Our backend runs on Render's managed platform with automatic OS patching.

No system is 100% secure. If we ever became aware of a personal data breach affecting users, we would notify the ICO within 72 hours under Article 33 UK GDPR, and notify affected users without undue delay if the breach posed a high risk to their rights.
